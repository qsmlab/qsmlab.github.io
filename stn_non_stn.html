What's actually different

An energy eigenstate ψₙ evolves only by an overall phase, Ψ(x,t) = ψₙ(x)e^(−iEₙt/ħ). Because probability density is |Ψ|², that phase cancels exactly — nothing observable moves, no matter how long you wait. That's what "stationary" means: stationary in probability, not in phase.

A superposition Ψ = Σ cₙψₙ e^(−iEₙt/ħ) mixes phases that rotate at different rates. Cross terms between pairs of states now survive in |Ψ|², oscillating at the Bohr beat frequencies ω_mn = (E_m − Eₙ)/ħ. The density sloshes back and forth inside the box, and quantities like <x>(t) genuinely move — this is the closest quantum mechanics gets to "classical-looking" motion.

Ehrenfest's theorem, and why it's checked live above

Ehrenfest's theorem says expectation values obey classical-looking equations of motion:

d<x>/dt = <p>/m     d<p>/dt = −<∂V/∂x>

Inside the box V(x)=0, so naively you'd expect <p> to be conserved. It isn't, once you mix states — it oscillates too, in exact lock-step with d<x>/dt. That's the point of the panel above: the box's infinite walls are already built into the boundary conditions ψₙ(0)=ψₙ(L)=0, and Ehrenfest's theorem holds throughout only because both <x> and <p> are computed from the same wavefunction that respects those walls. A single stationary state trivially satisfies both equations with everything constant at zero net motion; a superposition is the first place you actually see the classical-mechanics analogy come alive.

Try it: mix n=1 with n=2 (the largest <x> oscillation, since their coupling matrix element is largest), then add a third, more distant state and watch the motion turn less sinusoidal and more "wavepacket-like." Building a superposition of many eigenstates (n = 1…15, weighted like a Gaussian) is the standard route to a localized wavepacket whose <x>(t) genuinely bounces off the walls like a classical ball — a natural next extension of this page.

Where to go next: time-dependent perturbation theory (TDPT)

This page mixes stationary states with fixed, hand-chosen coefficients. The natural next step is to let an external field actually drive population between them. A standard, PIB-friendly choice is a spatially uniform oscillating field coupling to position, H'(t) = qE₀ x cos(ωt) — the same coupling that gives optical/Stark-type transitions.

  • First-order amplitude. Starting purely in state n, the amplitude to be found in state m after time t is c_m⁽¹⁾(t) = −(i/ħ)∫₀ᵗ H'_mn(t′) e^(iω_mn t′) dt′, with ω_mn=(E_m−Eₙ)/ħ and H'_mn = qE₀⟨φ_m|x|φₙ⟩cos(ωt′).
  • Selection rule. For the PIB, ⟨φ_m|x|φₙ⟩ is nonzero only when m and n have opposite parity — so a dipole-type drive only ever connects n=1↔2, 2↔3, 1↔4, etc., never 1↔3. That rule is already visible in the coupling used for <p> above.
  • Resonance. Transition probability P_{n→m}(t)=|c_m⁽¹⁾(t)|² grows appreciably only near ω ≈ |ω_mn|; away from resonance it stays small and oscillatory (first-order theory is only valid while it stays small).
  • Implementation route. Reuse this page's eigenbasis: instead of freezing the coefficients cₙ, integrate the coupled ODEs iħ ċ_m = Σₙ H'_mn(t) e^(iω_mn t) cₙ for a truncated basis (say n=1…15) with a simple RK4 stepper. Compare the exact numerical P_{n→m}(t) against the first-order TDPT formula to see where the weak-field/short-time approximation holds, and where it breaks down into Rabi-type flopping at/near resonance.
  • Limiting case. If the drive is switched on suddenly next to a dense set of levels, the long-time growth rate of first-order theory reduces to Fermi's golden rule Γ_{n→m}=(2π/ħ)|H'_mn|²ρ(E_m) — worth a mention if the box is later coupled to a continuum instead of a few discrete levels.
